# WordPress State of the Community 2026 — Evidence Base

**Source:** WP Open Community Collective (WPOCC), *State of the Community 2026*, published Aug 18, 2026. Press release: thewpocc.org. Survey fielded **May 27 – July 17, 2026**, with **431 respondents** answering **231 questions**. The WPOCC is a member-based organization supporting open-source contributors; it is **not affiliated** with the WordPress Foundation or the WordPress open-source project.

## The 11 findings (verbatim headlines)

1. **81.7% of respondents are positive about open source. 32.7% say the same about WordPress.**
2. **77.3% of respondents feel differently toward WordPress than they did two years ago. 95.5% of them feel worse.**
3. Three in four respondents are negative or unsure about WordPress's future (but they haven't all left yet).
4. **Governance and leadership is the problem the community reports most.**
5. **Participation is contracting, and the people stepping back have been in the community more than a decade.**
6. **Half the people doing community work don't consider themselves contributors.**
7. Belonging matters more than money to almost everyone (including the people being paid to participate).
8. The community uses AI every day. The community does not want AI in WordPress.
9. On AI and money, business owners are the only group that expects to win.
10. A third of respondents who have attended a WordPress event no longer do — and they have pulled back from everything else too.
11. **90% of respondents agree on the importance of security, web standards, open source values, and the WordPress software itself.**

## The methodological inventory (the reproducibility standard)

The WPOCC published, in full:
- The report plus the **aggregate data behind every number and quote**.
- The **survey instrument** (open to comment), the **methodology**, and a written account of the **limitations**.
- `findings-figures.csv` — every cited figure mapped to its sentence/chart and the file/lookup keys to verify it.
- The **full significance register** — all **499 statistically significant subgroup differences** across **1,374 tests**.
- Per-question tallies for every closed question, keyed to the instrument.
- Every 1–5 item broken out by every segment — **5,640 rows** — so any comparison can be rebuilt.
- Every derived variable used, published separately.
- **280 verbatim free-written responses** (with permission), including some criticizing the survey itself.

**Licensing:** aggregate data **CC BY 4.0** (commercial reuse fine, attribution required); report text **CC BY-NC 4.0**.

**Stated intent:** annual fielding with a consistent instrument so next year's report shows change, not just a snapshot.

## The three structural readings for A-Tech

### 1. The values-vs-governance dissociation
Finding 11's own framing is the survey's deepest insight: **"A community that had genuinely given up would not still be at 90% agreement on what is most important for the project."** The 81.7%-vs-32.7% gap plus 90% values agreement isolates *governance* — not the open-source model, not the software — as the failure layer. This is the sentiment-scale confirmation of the governance dynamics the library tracked in the 2024 WP Engine dispute (`open-source-agency-argument`).

### 2. Veteran contraction is the leading indicator
Finding 5 — the people stepping back have 10+ years tenure — inverts the usual churn pattern (newcomers leave first). When the long-tenure core pulls back, the knowledge and review capacity that onboarding depends on goes with it. Finding 10 compounds this: event-attrition correlates with pullback from *everything else*.

### 3. Contributor legibility
Finding 6 — half the people doing community work don't self-identify as contributors — is a measurement lesson for every ecosystem: commit-count-based contributor accounting undercounts the actual work base by a large margin. Any community-health dashboard built on contribution metrics alone will misread its own project.

## Honest caveats

- 431 self-selected respondents from a community under acute strain — measures the *sentiment of the engaged*, not the full WordPress user base (the WPOCC publishes its own limitations section).
- "Feeling worse" is retrospective self-report ("than two years ago") — recall effects apply.
- WordPress-specific; generalizing to other ecosystems is hypothesis, not finding.
- The WPOCC is independent of the WordPress Foundation but is a member-based organization with a stake in community health — independence is methodological (data-published), not institutional.
