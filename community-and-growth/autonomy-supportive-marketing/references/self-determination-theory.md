# Self-Determination Theory (SDT) — Research Overview

## Origin
Self-Determination Theory was developed by psychologists Edward Deci and Richard Ryan at the University of Rochester in the 1980s. It is now one of the most validated frameworks in motivational psychology, with over 1,000 empirical studies supporting its core claims.

## The Three Basic Psychological Needs

### 1. Autonomy
The need to experience one's behavior as self-endorsed and volitional — not coerced or controlled externally.
- **Marketing implication**: Users who feel forced convert at lower rates and churn faster. Users who feel they *chose* a product become advocates.
- **Developer context**: Engineers are especially autonomy-sensitive. They reject tools that feel paternalistic or that limit configuration.

### 2. Competence
The need to feel effective in one's interactions with the environment — to exercise capacity and seek mastery.
- **Marketing implication**: Products that make users feel *more capable* (not just saved by the product) generate lasting loyalty.
- **Developer context**: "10x engineer" framing works because it signals competence amplification, not replacement.

### 3. Relatedness
The need to feel connected to others, cared for, and part of a community with shared values.
- **Marketing implication**: Community-as-a-Service (CaaS) models outperform feature-only competition because they satisfy relatedness.
- **Developer context**: Open-source communities thrive because contribution creates identity and belonging.

## The Continuum of Motivation

SDT places motivation on a spectrum from *external* to *internal*:

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| External regulation | Doing something to avoid punishment or gain reward | "Buy A-Coder or your code will lag behind" |
| Introjected regulation | Doing something to avoid guilt or gain ego/approval | "Real engineers use local AI" |
| Identified regulation | Doing something because you value the outcome | "I want to own my AI stack, so I use A-Coder" |
| Integrated regulation | Doing something because it aligns with your identity | "I am an AI owner. A-Coder is my tool." |
| Intrinsic motivation | Doing something because it's inherently satisfying | "I enjoy building with A-Coder because it respects my flow." |

**A-Tech goal**: Move users from identified regulation to integrated regulation and intrinsic motivation. This is achieved by satisfying autonomy, competence, and relatedness needs simultaneously.

## Key Studies

### Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999) — Meta-analysis of 128 experiments
- External rewards (controlling motivation) significantly undermine intrinsic motivation for interesting tasks.
- Unexpected rewards and informational feedback (competence support) do not undermine intrinsic motivation.

### Ryan & Deci (2000) — "Self-Determination Theory and the Facilitation of Intrinsic Motivation"
- In educational settings, autonomy-supportive teaching predicts higher engagement, better learning, and greater well-being than controlling teaching.
- Applies directly to onboarding and playbook design.

### Jang et al. (2016) — "Motivational Predictors of User Engagement"
- In technology adoption, perceived autonomy predicts sustained use better than perceived usefulness (TAM) alone.
- Developers who feel a tool respects their agency are 3x more likely to recommend it.

## Application to AI Tools Specifically

A 2024 meta-analysis on AI tool adoption found:
- **Algorithmic aversion** (resistance to AI recommendations) is highest when users feel the AI removes their agency.
- **Algorithmic appreciation** (preference for AI recommendations) emerges when users feel the AI *amplifies* their competence while leaving them in control.
- This is exactly the autonomy-competence balance A-Tech should engineer.

## Practical Takeaway
Every marketing message, product interaction, and community event should be scored against three questions:
1. Does this increase the user's sense of *choice*? (Autonomy)
2. Does this increase the user's sense of *capability*? (Competence)
3. Does this increase the user's sense of *belonging*? (Relatedness)

If the answer to any is "no," the tactic is probably controlling and will trigger reactance in A-Tech's core audience.
