# Calm Technology Foundations

## Origin and History

Calm Technology was first articulated in the late 1990s by Mark Weiser and John Seely Brown at Xerox PARC. They envisioned a future where technology "fades into the background" instead of monopolizing mental space — a counterpoint to the prevailing assumption that more computing means more screen attention.

### Timeline

- **1990s:** Weiser & Brown publish Xerox PARC papers on ubiquitous computing and calm technology.
- **2007:** Designer and researcher Amber Case discovers the Xerox PARC papers while writing a thesis on the iPhone.
- **2010:** Case begins lecturing on Calm Technology worldwide, building on Weiser's original papers.
- **2014:** Case creates the Principles of Calm Technology and a dedicated website.
- **2015:** Case publishes the seminal book *Calm Technology: Principles and Patterns for Non-Intrusive Design* (CreateSpace). A five-year book tour follows.
- **2020:** Book translated into Japanese (BNN). Calm Technology collaborations inspire products including mui Lab, Inc.
- **2023:** Case joins the Metagovernance Project (Metagov) as Research Director. Calm Tech Institute begins incubation.
- **2024:** Calm Tech Institute launched at the WSJ Future of Everything Conference. Calm Tech Certified™ standard introduced.
- **2025:** Calm Tech Certified™ gains global adoption. Featured in Kyoto Journal, Wired Japan, Idler Magazine.
- **2026:** mui Lab's CES booth features Calm Technology to critical acclaim. Calm Tech Institute enters third year.

## The Eight Principles of Calm Technology®

1. **Require the smallest possible amount of attention.** Communicate information without pulling users out of their environment or task. Create ambient awareness through different senses.

2. **Inform and create calm.** A person's primary task should not be computing, but being human. Give people what they need to solve their problem, and nothing more.

3. **Make use of the periphery.** Calm technology moves easily from the periphery of attention to the centre and back. The periphery informs without overburdening.

4. **Amplify the best of technology and the best of humanity.** Design for people first. Machines shouldn't act like humans, and humans shouldn't act like machines. Amplify the best of each.

5. **Technology can communicate, but doesn't need to speak.** Consider whether your product needs voice or can use a different communication method. Think about how technology communicates status.

6. **Technology should work even when it fails.** Consider what happens when your technology fails. Does it default to a usable state or break down completely?

7. **The right amount of technology is the minimum needed to solve the problem.** Slim the feature set down so the product does what it needs to do and no more. Question every addition.

8. **Technology should respect social norms.** Technology takes time to introduce. Consider what social norms your product might violate. Leverage familiar behaviours to introduce new ones.

## Calm Technology vs. Smart Technology

| Dimension | Smart Technology | Calm Technology |
|-----------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Attention model | Demands focus via alerts | Operates in periphery; surfaces info only when necessary |
| Information delivery | Notifications, pop-ups, buzzes | Ambient signals: soft glows, haptics, sounds |
| User state | Interrupt-driven | Flow-supporting |
| Design goal | Maximize engagement / efficiency | Minimize cognitive burden |
| Failure mode | Breaks down completely | Defaults to usable state |
| Relationship to user | Tool is the object of attention | Tool fades; the true object of attention is revealed |

## The Silent Revolution (2026 Context)

The average person now spends 7+ hours per day in front of screens. Digital fatigue, attention-economy exhaustion, and mental health concerns are driving demand for technology that quiets down rather than competes for attention.

### Key 2026 Predictions

- **Shift from Attention Economy → Awareness Economy:** Value will be measured by integration quality, not screen time.
- **AI + IoT Integration:** Smart environments (homes, cities) will adapt quietly, anticipating needs without demanding interaction.
- **Policy & Regulation:** Governments may regulate notification overload and enforce digital well-being standards.
- **Sustainability:** Efficient devices require less energy, last longer, and mitigate e-waste and carbon footprints.

### Early Calm Technology Examples

- **Healthcare:** Ambient displays show patient status via colors instead of alarms.
- **Workspaces:** Smart lighting systems adjust to circadian rhythms.
- **Wearables:** Oura Rings and Fitbit trackers quietly monitor stress and health.
- **Ohm Health Resonance Lamp:** Calm Tech Gold Certified; borosilicate glass housing; warm stone-like biofeedback controller that reads heart rate and responds through light and sound.

## Ethical Considerations

- **Data Privacy & Security:** Quiet systems may collect sensitive data. Transparency is crucial — calm must not mean covert.
- **Balancing Calmness & Functionality:** Too subtle = ineffective. Too loud = not calm. The calibration is the craft.
- **Accessibility & Inclusivity:** Vibration alerts may not help those with impairments; inclusive design is vital. Calm technology must not exclude users who need explicit signals.

## Relevance to AI-Assisted Coding

The calm technology framework directly addresses the central problem of agentic coding: chat-based agents demand attention, break flow, and keep developers in an idle/interruptible holding pattern. Calm technology provides the principled alternative — AI that informs peripherally, stays pass-through to the code, and creates calm rather than engagement anxiety.

## Certification

Calm Tech Certified™ is a standard for aligning attention with technology through periphery engagement, tactility, material harmony, and durability. The certification provides a market signal for products that meet calm technology principles — a potential trust differentiator for A-Tech products.
