## The Conceptual Framework

AI fatigue is conceptualized as a **multi-dimensional form of strain** that arises from sustained interaction with AI systems. It is not a single feeling but a composite of distinct fatigue types, each with different antecedents and consequences.

### Five Dimensions

1. **Cognitive fatigue** — the mental effort required to understand, interpret, and verify AI-generated output. Includes the cognitive cost of detecting errors, hallucinations, and biases.

2. **Emotional fatigue** — the affective strain from frustration, disappointment, or unhealthy dependency on AI systems. Includes the emotional cost of unmet expectations.

3. **Social fatigue** — the strain from AI-mediated interactions replacing or mediating human-to-human contact. Relevant in AI customer service, AI companions, and AI-mediated communication.

4. **Operational fatigue** — the effort required to manage, configure, maintain, and troubleshoot AI tools. The "keeping the AI working" burden.

5. **Information overload fatigue** — the overwhelm from the volume, velocity, and variety of AI-generated content and outputs.

### Antecedents

**System factors:** output quality, reliability, transparency/explainability, interaction frequency, error recovery design.

**User factors:** AI literacy, prior expectations, personality traits, tolerance for ambiguity.

**Contextual factors:** task stakes (high-stakes amplifies verification burden), mandatory vs. voluntary use, time pressure, organizational culture.

### Consequences

- **Reduced AI engagement** — lower session frequency, shorter sessions, reduced feature usage
- **Discontinuance** — users abandon AI features entirely
- **Critical engagement** — continued use but with increasing skepticism, friction, and negative sentiment
- **Knowledge avoidance** — users actively prefer human sources over AI-generated information

## The 15-Item AI Fatigue Scale

The scale was developed through a multi-study validation process:
- **Study 1:** Initial item generation and scale development (30-item version)
- **Study 2:** Scale refinement to 15 items with psychometric validation
- Items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree)
- Measures AI fatigue and its association with AI engagement

The 15-item version is the recommended measurement instrument. The original 30-item version was used in Study 1 and refined down for practical use.

## Measurement Properties

- Validated across multiple samples
- Demonstrates convergent and discriminant validity
- Correlates with AI engagement metrics (negatively — higher fatigue → lower engagement)
- Suitable for longitudinal tracking (quarterly pulse surveys)

## Privacy-First Measurement Design

The scale is self-report — users voluntarily disclose their fatigue level. This is the privacy-first approach:

- **No biometric data** (EEG, GSR, eye tracking) — which would require bodily data collection
- **No behavioral surveillance** (typing cadence, scroll patterns, dwell time) — which would require continuous monitoring
- **No inference from usage logs** — which would require analyzing interaction patterns

The self-report approach aligns with A-Tech's data privacy values: the user owns their fatigue data and chooses when to share it.

## Design Implications

The framework suggests that AI fatigue is not a monolith — different users experience different dimensions. Product teams should:

1. **Measure all five dimensions** — not just a single "how tired are you of AI?" question
2. **Segment by dimension** — a user with high cognitive fatigue needs different interventions than one with high emotional fatigue
3. **Track longitudinally** — fatigue accumulates; quarterly measurement captures the trend
4. **Correlate with engagement** — fatigue is a leading indicator of disengagement and churn
5. **Design targeted countermeasures** — one-size-fits-all interventions miss the dimensional structure

## Academic Context Framing (arXiv:2605.23123)

The arXiv paper frames AI fatigue specifically in academic contexts — the strain from sustained use of AI tools in academic work (research, writing, analysis). This is relevant to Be Practical's educational framing: learners using AI for study and skill-building are a population at risk for academic AI fatigue, and the curriculum should include fatigue awareness and management.
