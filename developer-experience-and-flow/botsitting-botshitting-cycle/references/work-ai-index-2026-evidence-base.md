# Botsitting-Botshitting Cycle — Evidence Base

## Source

**Work AI Index 2026** (Glean Work AI Institute, June 10, 2026)

- **Title:** "Botsitting, Botshitting & the Hidden Human Labor of AI at Work"
- **Authors:** Rebecca Hinds (Work AI Institute), Mark Hoffman (Work AI Institute), Stephanie Baladi (Work AI Institute), Hancheng Cao (Emory University), Yong Suk Lee (University of Notre Dame), Paul Leonardi (UC Santa Barbara), Aruna Ranganathan (UC Berkeley), Jen Rhymer (University College London), Steven Rogelberg (UNC Charlotte), Bob Sutton (Stanford University), Yi Zhu
- **Sample:** 6,000 full-time digital workers (30+ hours/week) — US (n=3,000), UK (n=1,500), Australia (n=1,500)
- **Conducted:** December 2025 – January 2026
- **Methodology:** Nationally representative by age, gender, income. Survey screened out inattentive/speeder respondents with attention checks. Triangulated with interviews, case studies, third-party research, and anonymized aggregated telemetry from Glean Work AI platform. Driver analyses use logistic regression controlling for role, industry, demographics, employment status, organization size, and AI usage intensity. Odds ratios for binary outcomes; percentage-point differences for continuous.
- **Caveats:** Self-reported data (social desirability bias, recall bias). Sample skews higher on AI adoption, seniority, and digitally intensive sectors. Main findings hold after adjusting for controls.

## Key Definitions

**Botsitting (n.)** — The largely unrecognized, unbudgeted, and untracked labor of making AI usable — feeding it context, supervising its output, debugging its mistakes, and cleaning up after it.

**Botshitting (n.)** — The act of shipping AI-generated work that workers haven't verified, don't fully understand, or can't confidently stand behind.

**AI toggle tax (n.)** — The cumulative cost — in time, attention, and sanity — of switching between disconnected AI tools, apps, and systems, as the worker carries context, data, and intent from one tool to the next.

**Workslop** — AI-generated work content that masquerades as good work but lacks the substance to meaningfully advance a given task (Stanford/BetterUp researchers).

**Constructive deviance** — Rule-breaking by people who believe the outcome matters more than the process. Not reckless; committed to organizational goals but working around policy that can't keep up.

**Addition sickness** — The reflex to solve problems by piling more on top instead of subtracting what's already there (Bob Sutton).

**Tokenmaxxing** — The belief that more AI tokens signal more productivity. Meta employees competed on an internal leaderboard ranked by token usage; the "winner" averaged 281 billion tokens/month at compute cost of hundreds of thousands of dollars.

## Headline Statistics

- 87% of digital workers use AI at work
- 75% say AI makes them more productive
- 77% juggle multiple AI tools every week; 33% use four or more
- AI automates 27% of work output; workers expect 35% within a year; 57% want more
- Workers say AI saves 11 hours/week through automation
- Only 13% say their organization is performing significantly better because of AI
- 6.4 hours/week spent botsitting (37% of AI interaction time)
- 36% of AI time spent actually using AI to produce work
- 27% of AI time spent learning and building agents
- 36% of AI sessions "fail" outright, requiring full restart or substantial rework
- 69% of AI users admit to botshitting at work
- Only 0.5% of Claude users use Claude alone; average user runs 4 other AI tools
- 60% rerun the same prompt across multiple tools because first output wasn't good enough

## Botsitting Breakdown

| Component | Time/week | % of total AI time | Exhaustion multiplier |
|-----------|-----------|---------------------|----------------------|
| Feeding AI context | 2.3 hrs | 14% | 1.2× |
| Supervising outputs | 2.2 hrs | 13% | 1.1× |
| Debugging | 1.7 hrs | 10% | 1.4× |
| Other (cleanup, switching) | 0.2 hrs | 2% | 1.1× |

Context tax: for every 10% more time spent feeding AI context, workers are 25% more likely to report feeling worn out.

## Botshitting by Usage Intensity

| AI usage level | % who admit botshitting |
|----------------|------------------------|
| Light (1-19%) | 50% |
| Moderate (20-49%) | 73% |
| Heavy (50%+) | 82% |

Heavy users 64% more likely to botshit than light users. Workers using multiple AI agents 1.3× more likely to botshit (controlling for role, industry, usage intensity).

## Three Botshitting Forms

| Form | What it sounds like | % who do it |
|------|---------------------|-------------|
| Offloading understanding | "I don't understand what this says, but it looks right." | 41% deliver work they couldn't explain |
| Offloading judgment | "I know this isn't how we're supposed to do it, but it's faster." | 38% use unapproved tools; 37% use approved in noncompliant ways; 12% ship output they believe is wrong |
| Offloading responsibility | "That wasn't me, that was the AI." | 28% blame AI for mistakes they caused |

## Demographics

Who botshits most: Gen Z (+19%), men (+8%), managers (+6%).

When AI work fails: 40% blame AI; only 29% admit own fault. Heavy users 3.4× more likely than light users to blame the tool.

Moral disengagement: heavy AI users increasingly stop holding themselves accountable.

## The Three Paradoxes

### Productivity Paradox
- 75% say AI makes them more productive
- 63% say AI lets them do things they couldn't before
- Only 13% say organization performing significantly better
- 77% have corrected/redone AI-assisted work in past month; 30% weekly
- Driver: coordination neglect

### Judgment Paradox
- AI strips away disfluency cues (small frictions that prompt readers to slow down)
- Heavy users: 54% can't explain outputs (vs. 24% light); 16% use known-flawed output (vs. 6%); 41% blame AI (vs. 12%)

### Ownership Paradox
- Workers most afraid of being replaced use AI most
- 33% downplay AI's help; 33% exaggerate AI skills; 32% hide AI use
- 51% say AI has automated meaningful work they'd have preferred to keep; heavy users 62%
- Workers who admit botshitting 3.8× more likely to be actively job-hunting
- Frequent botsitters 73% more likely to be job-hunting

## Tool Sprawl and Toggle Tax

- Workers using multiple AI tools 35% more likely to report frequent botsitting
- Heavy AI users 111% more likely to report frequent botsitting than light
- MCP and APIs help connectivity but don't solve the context gap
- The worker becomes the integration layer

## Team Level

- AI adoption spreads peer-to-peer: 2.4× more likely when a leader uses it; 3.2× when a direct teammate does; 5.6× when a cross-functional teammate does
- 53% of workers trust AI as teammate; 52% find it easier than human colleagues; 61% say AI helps more day-to-day than manager
- Average worker collaborates with 3 AI agents alongside human teammates
- 17% have sent a bot to a meeting instead of showing up; 29% comfortable with AI involved in firing colleagues
- High-achieving managers delegate 32% more coordination to AI; reclaim time for coaching
- 74% of high achievers say AI helps more day-to-day than manager
- 44% say AI is fairer than their boss; climbs to 50% when manager has 10+ direct reports

Time in AI tools: transformative orgs 27% vs. non-transformative 49%. Context-rich orgs: 64% less worn out, 52% less likely to ship unexplainable work, 31% less likely to botshit, 9% less time botsitting.

When orgs measure quality alongside productivity: botshitting drops from 74% to 64%; 83% say AI improves quality (vs. 68% measuring productivity alone).

Employees who've seen CEO use AI: use it 67% more. Employees whose execs describe AI as teammate: 51% more likely to treat it as one, 65% more likely to report productivity gains.

Workers confident in org's AI strategy: 28% less likely to be job-hunting.

## Layoff Impact

When AI cited in layoffs: 73% worry AI will eliminate their role; 62% actively job-hunting; 64% hide AI use; 70% exaggerate AI skills; 71% deliver work they can't explain; 70% use unapproved tools; 45% of AI time botsitting; 94% botshit.
