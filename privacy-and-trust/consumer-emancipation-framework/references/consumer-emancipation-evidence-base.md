# Consumer Emancipation Framework: Evidence Base

## Study Details

- **Source:** Karichalil, S., & Kaul, A. (2026). "The Consumer Emancipation Framework: When AI-Enhanced Personalization Liberates and Oppresses Consumers." *Journal of Macromarketing*.
- **Journal:** *Journal of Macromarketing* (2026)
- **Framework type:** Novel, integrated theoretical framework (not a single empirical study). Synthesizes five established theories into a single diagnostic structure with seven propositions specifying boundary conditions.
- **Category:** Macromarketing — addresses the system-level, distributional, and structural dimensions of AI-enhanced personalization, not only the individual consumer–firm dyad.
- **Contribution:** First framework that (a) treats AI-enhanced personalization as *dual-pathway* (liberating vs. oppressing) rather than uniformly beneficial or harmful, (b) specifies the three constructs whose configuration selects the pathway, (c) specifies seven moderating boundary conditions, and (d) addresses distributional consequences for Global South and marginalized populations as a structural, not incidental, feature.

## The CEF Theoretical Framework

CEF reframes the central question of AI personalization. The dominant literature asks "does personalization work?" (firm-centric effectiveness) or "is personalization ethical?" (normative). CEF asks a third question: **when does personalization emancipate the consumer, and when does it oppress them?**

The framework is built on three moves:

1. **Reject the unitary framing.** Personalization is not one phenomenon. The same recommendation engine, with the same outputs, can be emancipatory for one consumer population and oppressive for another, depending on the configuration of three constructs and three moderators. The framework's job is to specify *when*.

2. **Locate agency as the dependent variable.** The outcome of interest is not engagement, conversion, or satisfaction — it is **consumer agency**: the consumer's capacity for self-determined consumption. Personalization that increases agency is emancipatory; personalization that degrades agency is oppressive.

3. **Make distributional consequences structural.** The framework refuses to treat unequal burdens on Global South and marginalized populations as externalities. P7 makes them a constitutive feature of the framework: the two pathways are not distributed randomly; structural inequalities shift populations toward the Oppressive Pathway as a baseline.

## The Three Key Constructs

### Construct 1: Algorithmic Transparency

**Definition:** The degree to which a consumer can understand, scrutinize, and contest the logic, inputs, and outputs of personalization algorithms — at a level that supports *actionable* beliefs, not merely disclosure.

**Key distinctions:**
- **Actionable intelligibility** (highest) — the consumer can form justified beliefs about how the system shapes their choices and can act on those beliefs (adjust, opt out, contest, switch).
- **Disclosure** (middle) — the firm publishes what data is used and how; the consumer can read it but may not be able to act on it.
- **Opacity** (lowest) — the consumer cannot form justified beliefs about the system at all.

**Theoretical anchor:** Nissenbaum's contextual integrity. Transparency is not maximal exposure; it is *contextually appropriate* intelligibility. A health-context data flow and a retail-context data flow have different appropriate-transparency norms. CEF imports this: transparency that violates contextual norms is not emancipatory transparency, it is contextual-integrity violation dressed up as openness.

**Role in the framework:** P1 makes algorithmic transparency a **necessary condition** for the Emancipatory Pathway. Its absence is **sufficient** for the Oppressive Pathway. This is the framework's strongest claim: no transparency, no emancipation, regardless of how good the personalization outputs are.

### Construct 2: Relationship Authenticity

**Definition:** The degree to which AI-mediated interactions preserve, rather than violate, the genuine relational character between consumer and firm (or consumer and product).

**Key distinctions:**
- **Augmented relationship** (authentic) — AI extends the genuine relational ground. The consumer–firm relationship has substance (real service, real accountability, real relational history) and AI makes it more responsive without impersonating it.
- **Simulated relationship** (inauthentic) — AI generates relational *signals* (empathy, rapport, personalization, "we know you") without the corresponding relational *substance*. This is parasocial mimicry: the consumer experiences relationship, the firm provides a synthetic performance of relationship.

**Theoretical anchor:** Authenticity theory. The framework distinguishes *being known* (authentic — the firm has real relational grounds for personalization) from *being profiled and performed at* (inauthentic — the firm generates the *appearance* of relationship from behavioral data without relational substance).

**The "authenticity trap":** A system can have high algorithmic transparency and still be oppressive if relationship authenticity is violated. Transparency about the fact that the AI is *simulating* a relationship does not make the simulation emancipatory. P2 makes authenticity a *mediator*: it carries the effect of transparency on agency, and its violation can override the protective effect of transparency.

**Role in the framework:** P2. Preserved authenticity mediates the transparency→agency link. Violated authenticity (parasocial mimicry, synthetic empathy, fabricated rapport) shifts the system toward the Oppressive Pathway *even when transparency is present*.

### Construct 3: Privacy–Personalization Tension

**Definition:** The net balance between cumulative privacy costs and personalization benefits, evaluated under contextual integrity norms.

**Privacy costs include:**
- Data extraction (volume, granularity, sensitivity of data taken)
- Surveillance (continuous monitoring, inferred states, predictive modeling of the consumer)
- Contextual boundary violations (data flowing across contexts in ways that violate contextual integrity norms)
- Attention and decision costs (the cognitive and temporal cost of being personalized at)

**Personalization benefits include:**
- Relevance (reduced search, better fit)
- Utility (functional improvement in the consumption task)
- Reduced information asymmetry (the consumer knows more about the choice space)

**Theoretical anchor:** The updated privacy calculus framework. The calculus is not a one-shot cost-benefit trade; it is cumulative and context-sensitive. Privacy costs accumulate across interactions and across contexts; a single personalization event may look net-positive while the cumulative calculus is net-negative.

**Role in the framework:** P3. The system shifts from the Emancipatory to the Oppressive Pathway when cumulative privacy costs exceed personalization benefits. Contextual integrity moderates this: a data flow that is appropriate in one context can make the calculus negative in another.

## The Emancipatory Pathway vs. The Oppressive Pathway

### The Emancipatory Pathway

**Configuration:** high algorithmic transparency + preserved relationship authenticity + low privacy–personalization tension (benefits exceed costs under contextual integrity).

**Outcome:** AI amplifies consumer agency. Personalization:
- Expands the consumer's choice space (more, better-organized options)
- Reduces information asymmetry (the consumer knows more, not less)
- Supports self-determined consumption (the consumer's choices remain their own)
- Widens the consumer's deliberative space (more capacity to reflect, compare, decide)

**Mechanism:**
- **Transparency** gives the consumer justified, actionable beliefs about the system — they can adjust, opt out, contest, or switch on the basis of understanding.
- **Authenticity** preserves the relational ground of trust — the consumer is *known* (on real relational grounds), not merely *profiled and performed at*.
- **Positive privacy calculus** means the consumer is not paying a hidden surveillance tax for relevance — the personalization benefit genuinely exceeds the privacy cost.
- **Posthumanist reading:** the Emancipatory Pathway is a *constructive human–AI assemblage* — AI extends rather than replaces consumer subjectivity. The consumer becomes more, not less, of an agent through the assemblage.

### The Oppressive Pathway

**Configuration:** absent algorithmic transparency + violated relationship authenticity + privacy costs exceed personalization benefits.

**Outcome:** AI degrades consumer agency through manipulation, surveillance, and dark patterns. Personalization:
- Narrows the consumer's choice space (filtered, steered, default-engineered)
- Increases information asymmetry (the system knows the consumer; the consumer does not know the system)
- Undermines self-determined consumption (choices are shaped without the consumer's understanding or consent)
- Shrinks the consumer's deliberative space (the consumer is acted upon, not acting)

**Mechanism:**
- **Opacity** denies the consumer the beliefs needed to contest the system. They cannot adjust what they cannot understand; they cannot opt out of what they cannot see.
- **Violated authenticity** replaces genuine relational trust with parasocial exploitation. The consumer experiences relationship; the firm provides a synthetic performance of relationship designed to extract.
- **Negative privacy calculus** means the consumer subsidizes relevance with their own data and attention. The personalization benefit is real but smaller than the cumulative privacy cost.
- **Psychological reactance:** consumers who *sense* (but cannot articulate) the manipulation experience reactance — the perception that their freedom is threatened. Reactance produces resistance, disengagement, or compensatory agency loss. The framework predicts that opaque manipulation produces more agency loss than transparent persuasion, precisely because the consumer cannot locate the threat to contest it.
- **Posthumanist reading:** the Oppressive Pathway is a *displacing assemblage* — AI replaces rather than extends consumer subjectivity. The consumer becomes less of an agent through the assemblage.
- **Bauman's liquid modernity:** the Oppressive Pathway is the dissolution of stable consumer subjectivity under fluid, opaque algorithmic power. The consumer's identity, preferences, and choices become liquid — continuously reconfigured by the algorithm without a stable ground from which to resist.

## The Seven Propositions

### Proposition 1 (P1): Transparency is necessary and its absence is sufficient

Algorithmic transparency is a **necessary condition** for the Emancipatory Pathway. Its absence is **sufficient** for the Oppressive Pathway. No amount of personalization benefit, relationship quality, or consumer literacy can compensate for the absence of actionable transparency — without it, the consumer cannot form the justified beliefs required for agency.

**Boundary condition:** "Transparency" here means *actionable intelligibility*, not disclosure. Publishing a privacy policy is not transparency in the CEF sense. The consumer must be able to form beliefs they can act on.

### Proposition 2 (P2): Authenticity mediates transparency→agency, and its violation overrides transparency

Preserved relationship authenticity **mediates** the link between algorithmic transparency and consumer agency. Violated authenticity — parasocial mimicry, synthetic empathy, fabricated rapport — **shifts the system toward the Oppressive Pathway even when transparency is present**. A firm can be transparent about the fact that it is simulating a relationship, and the simulation is still oppressive.

**Boundary condition:** Authenticity is about relational *substance*, not relational *signal*. AI-generated empathy without a real relational ground is inauthentic regardless of how warm it sounds.

### Proposition 3 (P3): The privacy calculus hinge

The privacy–personalization calculus shifts the system from the Emancipatory to the Oppressive Pathway when **cumulative** privacy costs exceed personalization benefits. The calculus is moderated by contextual integrity: data flows that violate contextual norms make the calculus negative even when the personalization utility is high.

**Boundary condition:** The calculus is cumulative and context-sensitive, not one-shot. A single personalization event can look net-positive while the cumulative calculus across interactions and contexts is net-negative.

### Proposition 4 (P4): Algorithmic literacy moderates all three constructs

Algorithmic literacy moderates the effect of all three constructs on agency:
- **Higher literacy** converts transparency into actionable agency (the consumer can act on what they understand) and lowers the personalization-utility threshold required for a positive privacy calculus (the literate consumer extracts more benefit from the same personalization).
- **Lower literacy** leaves consumers vulnerable: transparency they cannot act on is not emancipatory, and a manipulated privacy calculus they cannot evaluate is oppressive even when the outputs look beneficial.

**Boundary condition:** Literacy is not a panacea. P5 and P6 show that vulnerability and structural inequalities can override literacy's protective effect.

### Proposition 5 (P5): Consumer vulnerability amplifies the Oppressive Pathway

Consumer vulnerability — low literacy, dependency (on the firm, the platform, or the service), distress, cognitive load, or any condition that reduces the consumer's capacity for self-determined consumption — **amplifies** the Oppressive Pathway and **weakens** the protective effect of transparency. A system that is Emancipatory for a literate, low-vulnerability consumer can be Oppressive for the same system experienced by a vulnerable consumer.

**Boundary condition:** Vulnerability is situational, not fixed. The same consumer can be low-vulnerability in one context (buying a phone) and high-vulnerability in another (seeking health information under distress).

### Proposition 6 (P6): Structural inequalities shift the baseline toward oppression

Structural inequalities — the digital divide, market concentration, platform dependency, and asymmetries in data-protection infrastructure — **shift the baseline configuration** of personalization systems toward the Oppressive Pathway. Even well-designed personalization (high transparency, preserved authenticity, positive privacy calculus) has reduced emancipatory potential under structural inequality, because the consumer's exit option (switch, contest, refuse) is constrained by the structure.

**Boundary condition:** Structural inequality is a population-level moderator, not an individual one. P6 operates on the configuration of the system-in-context, not on the individual consumer.

### Proposition 7 (P7): Distributional consequences are unequally borne

The distributional consequences of the two pathways are **unequally borne**. Global South and marginalized populations disproportionately experience the Oppressive Pathway due to:
- **Weaker data-protection infrastructure** — lower regulatory floor, weaker enforcement, fewer recourse mechanisms.
- **Extractive data practices** — firms relocate or intensify extractive personalization in weaker-protection jurisdictions ("data colonialism").
- **Lower algorithmic-literacy baselines** — P4's protective effect is reduced where literacy investment is lower.
- **Higher consumer vulnerability** — P5's amplifying effect is stronger where economic, health, and informational vulnerability are higher.
- **Greater structural inequality** — P6's baseline shift is more severe where market concentration and platform dependency are more extreme.

**Boundary condition:** P7 makes distributional injustice a *constitutive* feature of the framework, not an externality. A CEF assessment that ignores the distributional burden is incomplete by construction.

## Moderation Summary

| Moderator | Effect on the Emancipatory Pathway | Effect on the Oppressive Pathway |
|-----------|--------------------------------------|----------------------------------|
| **Algorithmic literacy** (P4) | Strengthens — converts transparency into agency, lowers utility threshold | Weakens — literate consumers can detect and contest |
| **Consumer vulnerability** (P5) | Weakens — protective effect of transparency reduced | Amplifies — vulnerable consumers experience oppression more severely |
| **Structural inequalities** (P6) | Weakens — exit option constrained, emancipatory potential reduced | Strengthens — baseline shifts toward oppression |
| **Distributional / Global South** (P7) | Reduces — unequal access to emancipatory configurations | Increases — disproportionate oppressive burden |

## Mapping to Consumer / Firm / Product / Consumption Dimensions

CEF maps the two pathways onto four dimensions of the personalization system.

### Consumer-centric dimension
Concerns the consumer's agency, literacy, and vulnerability — the consumer-side conditions for emancipation.

| Pathway | Consumer-centric configuration |
|---------|----------------------------------|
| Emancipatory | Transparency the consumer can act on + literacy → agency expansion; vulnerability managed |
| Oppressive | Opacity the consumer cannot act on + vulnerability → agency degradation |

### Firm-centric dimension
Concerns corporate digital responsibility (CDR), algorithmic accountability, and the firm's relational practice.

| Pathway | Firm-centric configuration |
|---------|------------------------------|
| Emancipatory | CDR, algorithmic accountability, authentic relational practice, contextual-integrity-respecting data flows |
| Oppressive | Dark patterns, surveillance extraction, parasocial manipulation, contextual-integrity-violating data flows |

### Product-centric dimension
Concerns the personalization artifact itself — the recommendation engine, the AI-mediated interaction, the data pipeline.

| Pathway | Product-centric configuration |
|---------|-------------------------------|
| Emancipatory | Authentic augmentation, contextual-integrity-respecting data flows, transparent model logic |
| Oppressive | Synthetic empathy, fabricated rapport, privacy-violating data flows, opaque model logic |

### Consumption-centric dimension
Concerns the consumption act or choice that the personalization shapes.

| Pathway | Consumption-centric configuration |
|---------|-----------------------------------|
| Emancipatory | Expanded choice space, reduced information asymmetry, self-determined consumption |
| Oppressive | Narrowed choice, manipulated choice, agency loss |

## Distributional Consequences for Global South and Marginalized Populations

P7 is the framework's macromarketing contribution. The distributional analysis:

1. **Unequal exposure to the Oppressive Pathway.** Global South and marginalized populations are more likely to encounter personalization systems in their Oppressive configuration because the structural conditions (P6) and vulnerability conditions (P5) that select the Oppressive Pathway are more prevalent.

2. **Data colonialism.** Firms intensify extractive personalization in weaker-protection jurisdictions. The same firm can run an Emancipatory configuration in a strong-protection market and an Oppressive configuration in a weak-protection market — same algorithm, different pathway, selected by structural conditions.

3. **Literacy investment asymmetry.** Algorithmic literacy (P4) is the consumer's main protective resource, and it is unequally distributed. Where literacy investment is lower, transparency (P1) does not convert into agency even when it is present.

4. **Recourse asymmetry.** The exit/contest/switch options that make the Emancipatory Pathway work require functioning recourse mechanisms (regulators, courts, competition). Where these are weaker, even well-designed personalization has reduced emancipatory potential.

5. **Aggravating interaction.** P5, P6, and P7 interact: vulnerability, structural inequality, and distributional burden compound. A Global South consumer in a high-vulnerability context on a concentrated platform with weak recourse faces the Oppressive Pathway in its most severe form.

## Policy Implications

### Algorithmic accountability

P1 makes transparency a **necessary** condition, not a discretionary feature. Accountability regimes should:
- Enforce **actionable intelligibility**, not raw disclosure. A privacy policy is not transparency in the CEF sense.
- Treat opacity as a *per se* indicator of the Oppressive Pathway, not a neutral design choice.
- Require transparency that is **contextually appropriate** (Nissenbaum), not maximal exposure.

### Corporate Digital Responsibility (CDR)

P2 and the firm-centric dimension make authenticity and contextual-integrity-respecting data flows part of **corporate responsibility**, not just compliance. CDR should:
- Treat parasocial mimicry and synthetic empathy as **responsibility failures**, not just UX choices.
- Require firms to demonstrate that personalization rests on relational substance, not relational performance.
- Make contextual-integrity-respecting data flows a CDR obligation, not a privacy-law workaround.

### Distributional regulation

P7 requires that algorithmic accountability and CDR frameworks account for the unequal burden:
- Regulate **data colonialism** — firms should not run an Oppressive configuration in weak-protection jurisdictions while running an Emancipatory configuration elsewhere.
- Mandate **literacy investment** as part of accountability (P4 makes literacy the consumer's protective resource; regulators should fund it).
- Address **recourse asymmetry** — without functioning recourse, transparency does not convert into agency.

## Bauman's Liquid Modernity Analysis

CEF deploys Bauman's *liquid modernity* to analyze the Oppressive Pathway at the level of consumer subjectivity:

- **Liquid subjectivity.** Under opaque algorithmic power, the consumer's identity, preferences, and choices become liquid — continuously reconfigured by the algorithm without a stable ground from which to resist. The consumer cannot form a stable preference set because the system is continuously reshaping the choice environment.
- **Dissolution of the stable consumer.** The Emancipatory Pathway preserves a stable consumer subject who *uses* personalization as a tool. The Oppressive Pathway dissolves the stable consumer into a continuously reconfigured profile that the system *acts upon*.
- **The opacity–liquidity link.** Opacity (P1) is what makes subjectivity liquid: the consumer cannot stabilize their preferences against a system they cannot see. Transparency is the condition for re-solidifying consumer subjectivity — for giving the consumer a ground from which to contest the reconfiguration.
- **Distributional liquidation.** P7's distributional burden is, in Bauman's terms, a differential rate of liquidation: Global South and marginalized populations experience faster dissolution of stable consumer subjectivity because they have fewer resources (literacy, recourse, exit options) to re-solidify against the flow.

## Limitations

- **Theoretical framework, not empirical validation.** CEF is a synthesized theoretical framework. The seven propositions are theoretically derived boundary conditions, not empirically tested hypotheses. The framework is a research agenda as much as a finding.
- **Construct operationalization is underspecified.** "Actionable intelligibility," "relationship authenticity," and "cumulative privacy calculus" are conceptually clear but require operational measurement instruments before the framework can be applied as an audit tool. A-Tech's six-step diagnostic (in SKILL.md) is a pragmatic operationalization, not the framework's own.
- **Generalization beyond AI-enhanced personalization is untested.** The framework is built for AI-enhanced personalization. Whether it extends to non-AI personalization, to non-personalization AI (e.g., AI agents), or to broader algorithmic systems is an open question.
- **Posthumanist and Bauman lenses are interpretive, not predictive.** The posthumanist assemblage framing and the liquid-modernity analysis are interpretive resources, not testable mechanisms. They illuminate the Oppressive Pathway but do not generate falsifiable predictions.
- **Distributional analysis is structural, not jurisdiction-specific.** P7 identifies the distributional pattern; it does not provide jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction measurement. Applying P7 to a specific Global South context requires local data-protection infrastructure, literacy, and recourse assessment.
- **Moderator interactions are specified but not quantified.** P4–P7 interact (literacy, vulnerability, structural inequality, distributional burden compound), but the framework specifies the interactions qualitatively, not quantitatively. The aggravating interaction (P5 × P6 × P7) is identified but not parameterized.
- **Single-journal, single-framework.** CEF is one framework from one paper. It should be applied as a diagnostic lens, not as settled empirical consensus. Where it conflicts with other frameworks (e.g., a strictly firm-centric effectiveness view), the conflict is a research question, not a resolved dispute.
