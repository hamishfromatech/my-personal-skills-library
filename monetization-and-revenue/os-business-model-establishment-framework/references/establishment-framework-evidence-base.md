# Evidence Base: Open-Source Business Model Establishment Framework

## Source 1: "From Emergence to Establishment: Governance, Monetization, and the Evolution of Digital Business Models"

**Citation:** MDPI Administrative Sciences, vol. 16(7), article 304, June 2026. DOI: 10.3390/admsci16070304.

**Core argument:** Digital business models often emerge successfully but struggle to become established. Combining the business model literature with Transaction Cost Economics, the paper argues that establishment depends on the **co-evolution of governance and monetization** toward business model establishment.

**Key theoretical integration:**
- Business model literature: how value is created, delivered, and captured
- Transaction Cost Economics (Williamson 1979, 1981): governance structures economize on transaction costs when asset specificity, uncertainty, and opportunism are present
- The synthesis: establishment is not just about having a business model — it's about having governance and monetization that have *co-evolved* to align with the transaction attributes of the ecosystem

**Note:** Full text was not directly accessible (access denied at fetch time). The abstract, title, and theoretical positioning were confirmed via search results and the MDPI metadata. The framework's core concepts (co-evolution, governance-monetization alignment, TCE application) are consistently reported across multiple search result snippets.

## Source 2: "The Language of Platform Governance: An Economics-Based Scoping Review of SME Value Creation and Dependency in Digital Platform Ecosystems"

**Citation:** Dahlan, O. P. (2026). *Journal of Financial Literacy*, 1(1). Published 20 January 2026. Privietlab Research Center, Jakarta, Indonesia.

**Full citation:** Olivia Putri Dahlan. "The language of platform governance: An economics-based scoping review of SME value creation and dependency in digital platform ecosystems." Journal of Financial Literacy, Vol. 1 No. 1 (2026).

### Method
- Theory-led scoping review design (Arksey & O'Malley 2005; Levac et al. 2010; Tricoo et al. 2018)
- 40 DOI-verified, peer-reviewed sources
- Six conceptual streams: TCE & institutions; resource-based & dynamic capability; multi-sided market economics; digital platform architecture & governance; ecosystem strategy; SME digital transformation
- Abductive coding (theory and evidence developed together)
- Three coding passes: disciplinary home → economic mechanism → SME implications
- Inclusion: peer-reviewed, DOI-bearing, relevant to ≥1 stream, contributes mechanism/finding/method
- Exclusion: book chapters, practitioner articles without DOI, non-peer-reviewed working papers

### The Five Connected Mechanisms (Thematic Synthesis)

1. **Transaction-cost economizing:** Platforms reduce search, information, bargaining, and enforcement costs. But they *relocate* rather than eliminate transaction costs — compliance, switching, data-dependence, and bargaining costs may rise. Williamson's opportunism and asset specificity remain relevant: platform-specific investments create lock-in.

2. **Network-effect scaling:** Multi-sided market theory (Rochet & Tirole 2003; Parker & Van Alstyne 2005). Indirect network effects explain rapid scaling. But network effects create asymmetric power — once a platform is dominant, complementors' outside options weaken. Zhu & Liu (2018): platform owners may compete with complementors by entering successful spaces.

3. **Boundary-resource governance:** Boudreau (2010): granting access vs. devolving control. Ghazawneh & Henfridsson (2013): boundary resources as tools/rules/interfaces for third-party contribution. Tiwana et al. (2010): architecture and governance coevolve. Boundary resources are both enablers and control points.

4. **Ecosystem complementarities:** Adner (2017): ecosystems as aligned structures of partners. Jacobides et al. (2018): non-generic complementarities and role systems. SMEs rarely create value alone — dependence on payment providers, logistics, developers, platform policies. Bottlenecks elsewhere can damage SME performance.

5. **SME platform capability:** Barney (1991) RBV; Teece (2007) dynamic capabilities. Cenamor et al. (2019): entrepreneurial SMEs benefit from digital platform capability + network capability + ambidexterity. Jiang et al. (2023): digital platform capability → innovation performance. Access ≠ capability.

### The Three-Layer Framework

**Layer 1 — Economizing:** Platforms reduce selected transaction costs and mobilize network effects.
**Layer 2 — Orchestrating:** Platform owners coordinate access, rules, architecture, interfaces, ecosystem roles.
**Layer 3 — Capability-building:** SMEs develop resources and routines that transform participation into defensible value.

**All three layers must be present for sustainable value creation:**
- Economizing without orchestration → disorder
- Orchestration without SME capability → dependence
- Capability without governance awareness → vulnerability

### Key Distinctions

**Participation value vs. strategic value:**
- Participation value: benefits from access (visibility, transactions, infrastructure) — tied to platform
- Strategic value: benefits that strengthen beyond platform (customer knowledge, transferable routines, portable reputation)
- High participation + low strategic value = dependence

**Governance transparency as cross-cutting moderator:**
- Transparent rules make investment calculable
- Opaque governance increases uncertainty, discourages innovation
- Connects to North (1991): credible institutions lower uncertainty

**Temporal ordering of mechanisms:**
- Short run: transaction-cost economizing visible
- Medium horizon: network-effect scaling emerges
- Longer term: boundary-resource governance, ecosystem complementarity, capability-building become salient
- Short-term benefits may hide long-term strategic exposure

### Six Testable Propositions

1. SME platform participation improves performance when transaction-cost reductions exceed new governance costs
2. Digital platform capability → performance, moderated by governance transparency and data portability
3. Boundary-resource openness improves innovation when SMEs have absorptive capacity, but excessive control weakens value capture
4. Network effects increase growth opportunities, but platform-owner bargaining power moderates SME value share
5. Multi-platform strategies reduce dependency but increase coordination costs (requires ambidextrous routines)
6. Institutional safeguards (transparent rules, fair dispute resolution) strengthen trust and ecosystem resilience

### Managerial Implications

1. Governance due diligence before deep commitment (commissions, data access, ranking, dispute mechanisms, exit options)
2. Treat platform data as learning asset (but avoid dependence on non-exportable data)
3. Build network capability, not just platform capability
4. Strategic ambidexterity: exploit current platform while exploring alternatives
5. Assess whether platform-specific investments create defensible capabilities or deepen lock-in

### Policy Implications
- Platforms act as private regulators — opaque/unstable rules can harm SMEs
- Policymakers should encourage: transparency in rule changes, fair dispute resolution, data portability, interoperability, competition safeguards
- Credible governance strengthens ecosystem trust and complementor investment

### Limitations
- Theory-led scoping review, not full systematic review (no database counts, registered protocol, or meta-analysis)
- Single-author conceptual synthesis (no intercoder reliability)
- DOI requirement excludes books and practitioner analyses
- SMEs treated as broad category (micro/small/medium differ)
- Platform effects differ across sectors
