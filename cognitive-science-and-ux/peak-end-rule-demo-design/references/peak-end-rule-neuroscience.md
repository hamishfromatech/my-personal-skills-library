# Peak-End Rule: Foundational Research

## Original Research

**Kahneman, D., Fredrickson, B. L., Schreiber, C. A., & Redelmeier, D. A. (1993).** "When More Pain Is Preferred to Less: Adding a Better End." *Psychological Science*, 4(6), 401–405.

The foundational paper demonstrating that people prefer longer experiences with better endings over shorter experiences with worse endings—even when the longer experience contains more total pain. The colonoscopy study remains the most cited replication.

**Redelmeier, D. A., & Kahneman, D. (1996).** "Patients' memories of painful medical treatments: real-time and retrospective evaluations of two minimally invasive procedures." *Pain*, 66(1), 3–8.

Validated the Peak-End Rule in clinical settings. Patients who received a milder final phase of a painful procedure remembered the experience as significantly less unpleasant than those whose procedure ended abruptly—even though the former group experienced more total pain.

**Kahneman, D. (2011).** *Thinking, Fast and Slow*. New York: Farrar, Straus and Giroux.

Chapter on "Experienced Well-Being" situates the Peak-End Rule within the broader framework of System 1 (fast, emotional) and System 2 (slow, deliberative) thinking.

## Replications & Extensions

**Hoogerheide, V., Vink, M., Finn, B., Raes, A. K., & Paas, F. (2018).** "How to Bring the News … Peak-end Effects in Children's Affective Responses to Peer Assessments of Their Social Behavior." *Cognition and Emotion*, 32(5), 1114–1121.

Extended Peak-End Rule to peer assessments in educational settings. Found that even children as young as 8 show peak-end effects in memory formation.

**Zenko, Z., Ekkekakis, P., & Ariely, D. (2016).** "Can You Have Your Vigorous Exercise and Enjoy It Too? Ramping Intensity Down Increases Postexercise, Remembered, and Forecasted Pleasure." *Journal of Sport and Exercise Psychology*, 38(2), 149-159.

Exercise study showing that ending a workout with a cooldown (mild positive ending) dramatically improves memory of the experience versus abrupt endings.

## Neuroscience Foundations

**Amygdala-Hippocampus Interaction:**
- The amygdala assigns emotional salience to events
- The hippocampus encodes episodic memories
- Strong amygdala activation during peak moments creates durable memory traces
- Ending moments serve as "retrieval cues" that color the entire episodic memory

**Key Insight for Product Design:**
Because memory encodes peaks and ends rather than averages, a 10-minute experience with one brilliant peak and a warm ending will be remembered more favorably than a 60-minute experience that is consistently mediocre.

## Applications in Software and Sales

**Nielsen Norman Group (Kane, 2018):** "The Peak-End Rule: How Impressions Become Memories."
- Applied to UX design: users remember the most frustrating moment and the final screen
- Recommendation: design for emotional peaks in onboarding and strong positive endings in exit flows

**Braintrust Growth (Vujaklija, 2025):** "Leveraging the Peak-End Rule: Designing Sales Experiences That Leave a Lasting Impression."
- Sales-specific application: the emotional peak of a demo + the clarity of the close = memory
- Formula: Insight delivery / Problem magnification → Solution visualization → Unexpected value → Reinforced close

## The Decision Lab Summary
"The peak-end rule is a psychological heuristic that changes the way we recall past events. We remember a memory or judge an experience based on how we felt at its peak moments, as well as how it felt at the end."

**Practical advice from The Decision Lab:**
- Design products so that the most emotionally intense moments are positive
- End experiences on a high note
- Because people remember the peak and the end, improving these moments disproportionately improves overall satisfaction
