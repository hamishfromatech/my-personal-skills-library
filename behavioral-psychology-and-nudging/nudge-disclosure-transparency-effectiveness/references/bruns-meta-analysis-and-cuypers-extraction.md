# Nudge Disclosure Transparency Effectiveness — Evidence Base

## Source 1: Bruns, H., Fillion, A., Maniadis, Z., & Paunov, Y. (2025)
**Title:** "Comparing transparent and covert nudges: a meta-analysis calling for more diversity in nudge transparency research"
**Journal:** Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics, 102250
**DOI:** 10.1016/j.socec.2025.102350

### Study Design
- Meta-analysis of 23 publications comparing transparent to covert nudges
- 117 effect sizes pooled
- Domains: health, food, environment, finance
- 17 of 23 studies employed default nudges (limitation)
- Publication bias risk acknowledged

### Key Findings
- **Positive overall effect** of disclosure on behavioral outcomes (transparent nudges more effective than covert)
- For non-behavioral outcomes (perceptions, intentions): no significant difference
- Authors warn of potential publication bias
- Call for more diversity in nudge types studied (beyond defaults)

### Theoretical Context
- Bruns & Paunov (2021) identify nine distinct disclosure content elements
- de Ridder et al. (2022) consolidate into four fundamental content elements aligned with Lasswell (1948): presence (what/where/when), purpose (why), mechanism (how), source (who)
- The source element has no examples in the nudge disclosure literature (Bruns & Paunov 2021)

### Related Prior Findings
- Loewenstein et al. (2015): "Warning: you are about to be nudged" — early transparency study
- Bruns et al. (2018): "Can nudges be transparent and yet effective?" — foundational study, combined disclosure did not strengthen nudge
- Michaelsen (2024): methodological critique, calls for heterogeneity in nudge types studied

## Source 2: Cuypers, R., Raymaekers, P., & Van de Walle, S. (2026)
**Title:** "Unpacking transparency in nudging: the impact of different disclosure messages on nudge effectiveness and perceived autonomy"
**Journal:** Behavioural Public Policy (FirstView), pp. 1-39
**DOI:** 10.1017/bpp.2026.10037
**Open Access:** CC BY 4.0
**Preregistered:** https://osf.io/yr7sw
**Ethics:** KU Leuven SMEC G-2023-7072-R2
**Funding:** Research Foundation Flanders (FWO) PhD Fellowship

### Study Design
- **Vignette survey experiment, N=1,916** (Flemish representative sample via Bilendi)
- Power analysis: minimum N=1,852; achieved N=1,916 (sufficient at power=0.8, α=0.05, f²=0.007)
- Sample: 47.1% men, mean age 52.94 (SD=17.43), matching 2024 Flemish population
- Six experimental groups: Control, Covert Nudge, Presence disclosure, Purpose disclosure, Mechanism disclosure, Combined disclosure
- Nudge type: **salience nudge** (sustainable food menu — dishes placed on top, colored font, 'chef's choice' heading, eco-labels A-E)
- Four disclosure types tested: presence (type interference), purpose, mechanism, combined (all three)
- Outcome 1: nudge effectiveness (% choosing sustainable dish)
- Outcome 2: perceived autonomy (13 items, 5-point Likert, combining State Reactance Scale + BPNES autonomy subscale + 2 new positive items; Cronbach's α=0.72 overall, 0.84 for reactance, 0.62 for BPNES)
- Equivalence testing (TOST) applied to all non-significant results (bounds: ±0.076 for effectiveness, ±0.108 for autonomy)
- Restricted sample analysis (excluding manipulation check failures, n=1,059) as robustness check

### Key Results

#### Nudge Effectiveness
- Salience nudge successfully increased sustainable choices: **+10.4 percentage points** (Wald(1)=7.50, p=0.006)
- Control: 33.0% sustainable choice
- Covert Nudge: 43.4%
- Presence disclosure: (not significant vs. control)
- Purpose disclosure: significant vs. control
- Mechanism disclosure: significant vs. control
- Combined disclosure: 46.4% (highest, +13.4 pp vs. control)
- **No disclosure group differed significantly from the covert Nudge group** (H1 not supported)
- TOST: Mechanism disclosure and Nudge group were statistically equivalent (Z_low=2.01, p=0.022; Z_high=-1.80, p=0.036)

#### Perceived Autonomy
- Nudge and Presence groups reported **significantly lower autonomy** than control
- Nudge reduced perceived autonomy by **2.4 percentage points** (0.09 on 5-point scale)
- Control: M=3.74; Nudge: M=3.66; Presence: M=3.65
- Purpose, Mechanism, Combined: not significantly different from control
- **No disclosure group differed significantly from the covert Nudge group** (H2 not supported)
- TOST: Presence, Mechanism, Combined groups all statistically equivalent to Nudge group
- Restricted sample contradicts: only Mechanism group differed significantly from both Control (p=0.024) and Nudge (p=0.014)

#### Manipulation Check Failures
- Nudge awareness check failure: 40.8%
- Disclosure awareness check failure: 40.0%
- Total: 857 failed respondents
- High failure rates consistent with prior transparent nudging research

### Covariates (significant)
- Nudge effectiveness: female, left-leaning ideology, financial security → more likely sustainable choice; recent vegetarian meals → less likely (surprising)
- Perceived autonomy: right-leaning → lower; frequent vegetarian → lower; sustainability/taste/nutrition importance → higher; financial stability → higher

### Hypotheses Tested (all from Table 1)
| Hypothesis | Direction | Result |
|-----------|-----------|--------|
| H1a: Presence decreases effectiveness | − | Not supported |
| H1b: Purpose increases effectiveness | + | Not supported |
| H1c: Mechanism increases effectiveness | + | Not supported |
| H1d: Combined increases effectiveness | + | Not supported |
| H2a: Presence decreases autonomy | − | Not supported |
| H2b: Purpose increases autonomy | + | Not supported |
| H2c: Mechanism increases autonomy | + | Not supported |
| H2d: Combined increases autonomy | + | Not supported |

### Theoretical Contributions
1. First direct comparison of presence, purpose, and mechanism disclosures
2. Extends transparency research beyond default nudges to salience nudges
3. Integrated three autonomy conceptualizations (Vugts et al. 2020): freedom of choice, agency, self-constitution
4. Added two positively framed autonomy items (nudges can be autonomy-enhancing, not just harmful)
5. Among the first to empirically test whether disclosures offset reduced perceived autonomy

### Limitations (authors' own)
1. Single combination of salience techniques treated as one nudge — generalizability unclear
2. Controlled online vignette rather than field experiment; demand effects possible despite random-lottery mitigation
3. Did not directly measure perceived transparency increase from disclosures
4. Autonomy subscale reliability was lower than expected (BPNES α=0.62 vs. 0.76-0.89 in prior studies)
5. High manipulation check failure rates (~40%) limit disclosure reach

### Practical Implications (from Discussion)
- Disclosures can be implemented without reducing nudge effectiveness
- Different disclosure content elements have limited differential impact
- Disclosures are not sufficient to mitigate autonomy concerns
- Noticeability of disclosures is a critical practical issue
- The combined disclosure produced the highest number of sustainable choices (descriptive, not statistically significant vs. covert)
