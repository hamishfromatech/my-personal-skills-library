# Nudge Invisibility: Evidence Base & Methodology

## Source

Fisher, M. & Oppenheimer, D. M. (2026). "When a nudge becomes invisible: How behavioral interventions prompt metacognitive miscalibration." *International Journal of Research in Marketing*, Elsevier, vol. 43(1), pages 113-130. DOI: 10.1016/j.ijresmar.2025.03.006

## Study Design

Ten studies, total N = 5,395. Nudge types tested: reminders, defaults, decision aids.

## Key Findings (from abstract and RePEc listing)

1. Consumers attribute the positive effects of nudges to themselves rather than to the nudge.
2. Consumers underestimate the extent to which their behaviors are influenced by external aids.
3. The effect occurs because nudges create ambiguity, leading individuals to mistakenly attribute their improved outcomes to their own abilities rather than the nudge.
4. The miscalibration is metacognitive — it concerns knowledge about one's own cognitive performance and its causes.

## Theoretical Contribution

This is the first systematic evidence that nudges carry a hidden cost to user self-knowledge, distinct from known concerns about autonomy and manipulation. It extends self-serving attribution bias (well-established in social psychology) to the specific case of nudge-induced improvements.

## Keywords (from source)

Nudge; Attribution; Ambiguity; Metacognition

## Implications for Nudge Design

- Transparency at the *moment of action* is necessary but insufficient — the metacognitive distortion occurs at the *moment of success*, when the user evaluates the outcome.
- Attribution visibility must be surfaced at outcome time, not just action time.
- The effect is universal (not a individual failing), so calibration interventions should be framed normalization, not correction.

## Relationship to Existing Behavioral Science

- Extends self-serving attribution bias (Campbell & Sedikides, 1999) to nudge contexts.
- Connects to metacognition literature (Flavell, 1979) — specifically, metacognitive *monitoring* (assessing one's own performance) is what becomes miscalibrated.
- Relates to the Dunning-Kruger effect but is distinct: Dunning-Kruger concerns competence estimation; nudge invisibility concerns *causal attribution* for successful outcomes.

## Limitations Noted

- Studies are consumer-behavior focused; direct replication in developer/IDE contexts is needed.
- The effect size across all ten studies is not detailed in the available abstract; the full text (paywalled) would be needed for precise effect sizes.
- Cultural variation in self-attribution patterns is not addressed in the available materials.
