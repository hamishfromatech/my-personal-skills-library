# Paunov & Grüne-Yanoff (2026): Boosts vs. Nudges — Public Preference Findings

## Study Details

- **Authors:** Yavor Paunov, Till Grüne-Yanoff
- **Published:** February 2026, Energy Policy, Volume 209, Part A, Article 114953
- **Title:** "Boosts vs nudges: perceived effectiveness and criticism shape preferences for sustainable behavioural policies"
- **Publisher:** Elsevier
- **DOI:** Available via ScienceDirect (S0301421525004604)

## Core Research Question

What shapes public preference between boosts and nudges for sustainable behavioral policies? Is it the underlying theory, the perceived effectiveness, or the criticism each intervention type receives?

## Key Findings

### Finding 1: Perceived Effectiveness Drives Preference

People prefer the intervention type they perceive as more effective. This is not the same as the intervention that IS more effective — it's the intervention perceived as effective.

**Implication:** The theoretically superior intervention (which may be a boost for durable behavior change) only wins public preference if it is also perceived as effective. Communication of effectiveness matters as much as the effectiveness itself.

### Finding 2: Perceived Time/Effort Costs Reduce Boost Preference

Among the criticism variables, higher perceived time/effort costs reduced the odds of choosing a boost (vs a nudge):
- **B = −0.568**
- **p = .007**
- **OR = 0.57** (95% CI)

**Interpretation:** For every unit increase in perceived time/effort cost, the odds of choosing a boost decrease by 43%. Boosts are structurally disadvantaged by the perception that they require more user effort — even though this effort produces more durable results.

### Finding 3: Criticism Reduces Preference for the Criticized Type

Criticism of an intervention type reduces preference for that type:
- Nudges lose preference when criticized (the 2026 reckoning — Chater & Loewenstein's "It's On You" critique, the replicability crisis)
- Boosts lose preference when perceived as effortful

**Implication:** The nudge-boost debate is not just theoretical — it's a perception battle. The intervention that is both effective and un-criticized wins public preference. Currently, nudges face more criticism (the reckoning) but boosts face the effort perception.

## Statistical Summary

| Variable | Effect on Boost Preference | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| Perceived effectiveness of boost | Positive (increases boost preference) | Significant |
| Perceived time/effort cost of boost | Negative (B = −0.568, OR = 0.57) | p = .007 |

| Criticism of boost | Negative (decreases boost preference) | Significant |

## The Perception Trap

Boosts face a structural perception problem:

1. **The effort perception:** Boosts require teaching a skill, which is perceived as effortful. Nudges just change the default, which is perceived as effortless.
2. **The speed perception:** Nudges appear to work faster (immediate behavior change). Boosts take time (competence building).
3. **The convenience perception:** Nudges are done TO the user. Boosts require the user's active participation.

**The irony:** The very features that make boosts theoretically superior (durability, transferability, agency-building) are the features that make them harder to "sell" to the public. The intervention that builds lasting competence loses the perception battle to the intervention that changes one decision.

## Implications for Behavioral Policy Design

### 1. Communication Is as Important as the Intervention

The theoretically superior intervention only works if people accept it. Communication must:
- Reframe effort as investment
- Reframe speed as sustainability
- Reframe participation as empowerment

### 2. The Hybrid Strategy

The optimal approach is hybrid:
- **Nudge to start:** Overcome the effort/speed perception barrier
- **Boost to sustain:** Build the durable competence
- **Self-nudge to maintain:** User becomes their own choice architect

This three-stage approach captures the strengths of both: the perception advantage of nudges (fast, effortless) and the theoretical advantage of boosts (durable, transferable).

### 3. The Criticism Asymmetry

Currently, nudges face more criticism (the 2026 reckoning) than boosts. This creates a window for boosting-first strategies — but only if the effort perception is managed. A-Tech's boosting-first approach is well-positioned, but must communicate effectiveness to overcome the effort perception.

### 4. Context Dependency

The finding implies context-dependent intervention choice:

| Context | Perceived Effort Tolerance | Preferred Intervention |
|---|---|---|
| One-time, low-attention | Low | Nudge |
| High-stakes, deliberate | Higher (if communicated as investment) | Boost |
| Time-constrained users | Low | Nudge first, boost later |
| Autonomy-valuing users | Higher (participation is the value) | Boost |
