# Cross-Domain Spillover Effects of a Social-Comparison Nudge — Evidence Base

## Primary Source

**Ling, Q., Liu, Y., & Xu, H. (2026).** "Cross-domain spillover effects of a social-comparison nudge: Evidence from a natural field experiment on household waste sorting." *Journal of Environmental Psychology*, Vol. 110, 102926. DOI: 10.1016/j.jenvp.2026.102926.

---

## Study Design

### Overview

A two-year natural field experiment evaluating whether a HER-type (Home Energy Report-type) social-comparison nudge for household waste sorting — the **Home Waste-Sorting Report (HWSR)** — produces cross-domain spillover effects on residential water, electricity, and gas consumption.

### Setting

- **Location:** Guali Town, Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province, China
- **Duration:** Two years of continuous treatment and measurement
- **Scale:** 1,788 households across 14 residential communities
- **Design:** Opt-out (households receive the HWSR unless they actively decline), not opt-in

### The Intervention: Home Waste-Sorting Report (HWSR)

The HWSR is modeled on the Opower-style Home Energy Report (HER), one of the most widely studied social-comparison nudges in the behavioral science literature. The report provides:

- **Social comparison feedback:** Each household receives periodic reports comparing their waste-sorting compliance to that of their neighbors (similar to the HER's social comparison of energy use).
- **Descriptive norm information:** Reports include information about the average waste-sorting behavior of comparable households in the community.
- **Behavioral tips:** Actionable suggestions for improving waste-sorting compliance.

The HWSR targets waste-sorting behavior as the **primary (target) domain**. The study measures effects on water, electricity, and gas consumption as **spillover (non-target) domains**.

### Why This Design Is Methodologically Strong

| Feature | Detail | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Opt-out design** | All households receive the HWSR unless they actively decline | Avoids the self-selection bias of opt-in designs, where motivated participants inflate effect estimates. Preserves external validity. |
| **Longitudinal administrative data** | Water, electricity, and gas consumption measured from utility records over two years | Objective behavioral measures, not self-report. Eliminates social desirability bias and demand characteristics that plague self-reported spillover behavior. |
| **Two-year duration** | Continuous treatment and measurement over 24 months | Allows detection of both immediate and persistent spillover effects. Short studies miss delayed or slow-building spillovers. |
| **1,788 households, 14 communities** | Substantial sample with community-level variation | Sufficient power for community-level heterogeneity analysis, which is where the key findings live. |
| **Pre-treatment baseline** | Consumption baselines established before the nudge | Controls for pre-existing differences across communities in water, electricity, and gas use. |
| **Natural field experiment** | Intervention deployed in real communities by real authorities, not in a lab | High ecological validity. Participants behave as they normally would, not as study subjects. |

## Results

### Headline Finding: Null Average Cross-Domain Spillovers

**On average, the HWSR nudge had no significant effects on water, electricity, or gas consumption.**

The pooled, average cross-domain spillover effect across all 1,788 households and 14 communities was not statistically significant for any of the three spillover domains. This means that if you only look at the average effect — as many evaluations do — you would conclude that the waste-sorting nudge does not spill over to other resource-conservation behaviors.

This null average is consistent with the broader nudge-effectiveness literature (see the second-order meta-analysis finding of d ≈ 0.004 after bias correction), and it pushes back against the optimistic assumption that pro-environmental nudges automatically generalize across domains.

### The Critical Heterogeneity: Institutional Context Determines Spillover Direction

The null average conceals substantial heterogeneity across communities. When the researchers decomposed the effects by community-level institutional context, a clear pattern emerged:

#### Positive Spillovers: No Financial Incentives + Low Social Capital

Communities that met two conditions showed **positive and persistent** cross-domain spillovers:

1. **No financial incentives for waste sorting** — these communities did not pay households for sorting waste. The nudge was the primary behavioral prompt.
2. **Low social capital** — these communities had weaker social networks, lower interpersonal trust, and less established waste-sorting norms.

In these communities, the HWSR nudge produced significant reductions in water, electricity, and/or gas consumption — spillover behaviors that were never targeted by the intervention.

**Why?** The mechanism is **internalization via moral utility alteration**. In the absence of financial rewards, households cannot attribute their waste-sorting behavior to an external payment. With weak pre-existing social norms, the nudge's social-comparison information is the primary normative signal. Under these conditions, the nudge shifts the household's self-perception: "I am someone who sorts waste because I care about the environment." This altered moral self-concept then generalizes to other pro-environmental behaviors (water, energy, gas conservation) through the direct moral utility and self-related beliefs channels.

#### Suppressed Spillovers: Financial Incentives + High Social Capital

Communities that met two conditions showed **suppressed** (absent or negative) cross-domain spillovers:

1. **Financial incentives for waste sorting** — these communities paid households for sorting compliance.
2. **High social capital** — these communities had dense social networks, strong trust, and established waste-sorting norms.

In these communities, the HWSR nudge did not produce significant spillover effects on water, electricity, or gas consumption, despite the nudge being present.

**Why?** The mechanism is **external justification undermining internalization**. When households receive financial payment for sorting waste, they attribute their behavior to the reward ("I sort waste because I get paid"), not to an internalized environmental value. The financial incentive provides an external justification that crowds out the moral utility channel. High social capital reinforces this: strong existing norms mean the social-comparison nudge adds little new normative information, and the dominant attribution is to the incentive + existing social expectation, not to personal environmental identity. Without the identity shift, there is no cross-domain generalization.

### Persistence

The spillovers observed in the no-incentive / low-social-capital communities were **persistent** — they endured across the two-year treatment period, not just at the beginning. This is important because it rules out a "novelty effect" explanation (where the nudge temporarily raises attention across all domains, which then fades). The persistence is consistent with the moral utility mechanism: once the self-concept shifts, the effect on adjacent behaviors is durable.

### Cost-Effectiveness Implications

The study emphasizes that **even small spillover effects can substantially affect the cost-effectiveness calculation** for the nudge. The reasoning:

- The marginal cost of the HWSR is near zero (it is a report, not a subsidy or infrastructure investment).
- The direct benefit (improved waste sorting) may be modest in monetary terms.
- But if the nudge also produces even small reductions in water, electricity, and gas use, the combined monetary value of these multi-domain resource savings — aggregated across 1,788 households over two years — can substantially improve the benefit-cost ratio.
- This means that evaluations which measure only the target domain (waste sorting) will **underestimate** the total social benefit of the nudge in contexts where positive spillovers arise.
- Conversely, in contexts where spillovers are suppressed (financial incentives + high social capital), the cost-effectiveness case rests entirely on the direct effect, which may be insufficient to justify the intervention.

## The Four-Mechanism Taxonomy

The study draws on and contributes to a theoretical framework with four classes of mechanisms through which cross-domain spillovers can occur. These are not mutually exclusive; multiple mechanisms may operate simultaneously, and their net effect determines the observed spillover direction.

### 1. Direct Moral Utility Alteration

The nudge changes the actor's moral self-concept or the moral weight they place on pro-environmental behavior. If the nudge causes the actor to internalize an environmental identity ("I am someone who cares about the environment"), this identity generalizes to other pro-environmental behaviors beyond the nudged domain.

- **Direction:** Positive when internalization occurs; suppressed when external justification (e.g., financial incentives) prevents internalization.
- **Context dependence:** High. This is the mechanism that the study's institutional context findings directly illuminate. Without financial incentives and with low social capital, internalization is maximal. With financial incentives and high social capital, external justification dominates and internalization is crowded out.
- **Persistence:** High. Identity shifts, once achieved, are durable — consistent with the study's finding of persistent spillovers.

### 2. Mechanical Complementarities

The nudged behavior and the spillover behavior share a physical, structural, or economic linkage. Performing the nudged behavior mechanically makes the spillover behavior easier, cheaper, or more likely — without any psychological mediation.

- **Direction:** Usually positive (complementarity) or neutral. Can be negative if the nudged behavior consumes resources needed for the spillover domain.
- **Context dependence:** Low. Mechanical complementarities operate regardless of institutional context. They are structural, not psychological.
- **Example (not from this study):** A nudge to install a low-flow showerhead mechanically reduces water use (direct effect) and may also reduce gas use (less water to heat) — this is a mechanical complementarity, not a psychological spillover.
- **Important distinction:** The study's spillover findings on water, electricity, and gas are **not** mechanical complementarities. Waste sorting has no physical linkage to water or energy use. The spillovers (where observed) are psychological, driven by the moral utility and identity mechanisms.

### 3. Resource Competition

The nudged behavior consumes resources (time, money, cognitive bandwidth, physical effort) that are then unavailable for the spillover domain. This produces negative spillovers.

- **Direction:** Negative. The actor "uses up" their resources on the nudged behavior and has less available for other pro-environmental behaviors.
- **Context dependence:** Moderate. The magnitude depends on how resource-intensive the nudged behavior is and how resource-constrained the actor is.
- **Example:** If waste sorting requires significant time and effort, households may reduce their effort on energy conservation ("I've done my environmental duty for the day").
- **In this study:** Resource competition may partially explain the null average — in some communities, the time/effort cost of waste sorting may offset any positive moral-utility spillover, producing a net zero.

### 4. Self-Related Beliefs and Identity

The nudge shifts the actor's self-perception, self-efficacy beliefs, or social identity in ways that carry across domains. This is related to but distinct from the moral utility mechanism: moral utility is about the moral valuation of the behavior; self-related beliefs are about the actor's perception of their own capability and identity.

- **Direction:** Positive if the nudge builds self-efficacy ("I can make a difference on environmental issues") or activates a pro-environmental social identity. Negative if the nudge triggers moral licensing ("I've already done my part, so I don't need to conserve energy too").
- **Context dependence:** High. Whether self-efficacy or moral licensing dominates depends on the framing, the actor's baseline self-perception, and the institutional context.
- **Persistence:** Moderate to high for identity shifts; low for moral licensing (which tends to be a one-off justification, not a durable identity change).

## Limitations

| Limitation | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Single geographic setting** | Guali Town, Zhejiang Province. The institutional context (financial incentives, social capital configuration) is specific to this Chinese town. Generalization to other cultural, political, or economic contexts requires replication. |
| **Waste sorting as the target domain** | The spillover dynamics may be specific to waste sorting (a highly visible, community-level behavior). Nudges targeting less visible behaviors (e.g., energy use, which is private) may produce different spillover patterns. |
| **Social capital measurement** | The study classifies communities as high vs. low social capital, but the specific measurement approach and validation are not detailed in the available summary. Social capital is a multi-dimensional construct (trust, networks, norms) and its operationalization matters. |
| **Two-year window** | While two years is substantial, longer-term persistence (5+ years) is unknown. The persistence finding is bounded by the observation window. |
| **Spillover domains limited to utilities** | Water, electricity, and gas are the only spillover domains measured. Other plausible spillover domains (transportation, food consumption, purchasing behavior) are not assessed. |
| **Opt-out design ethics** | Opt-out designs raise ethical questions about consent and autonomy. While methodologically strong, the ethical acceptability depends on the local regulatory and cultural context. |
| **Interaction effects not fully specified** | The 2×2 institutional context framework (incentives × social capital) is a simplification. Real communities vary on more dimensions, and the interaction of these dimensions may be more complex than a four-cell table suggests. |
| **Null average interpretation** | The null average does not mean "spillovers never happen." It means the average across heterogeneous contexts is zero. Policy makers who read only the headline may incorrectly conclude that spillovers are not worth measuring or incorporating into cost-effectiveness analyses. |

## A-Tech Alignment

| A-Tech Value | Alignment |
|---|---|
| **Open-source AI** | The study's methodology — objective administrative data, longitudinal measurement, community-level heterogeneity analysis — is directly applicable to open-source behavioral analytics tools. An open-source library for cross-domain spillover analysis could operationalize the four-mechanism taxonomy and the institutional context assessment framework for any behavioral intervention dataset. |
| **Data privacy** | The study uses utility consumption records, which are sensitive but non-biometric. The methodology works with low-privacy-sensitivity data (aggregate consumption, not individual behavior traces). For A-Tech products, spillover analysis can be conducted on anonymized, aggregated behavioral metrics without invasive personal tracking. |
| **Financial freedom** | The cost-effectiveness insight — that small spillovers can substantially shift the benefit-cost ratio for low-cost nudges — directly supports the financial case for behavioral interventions in A-Tech products. A near-zero-cost nudge that produces even small cross-domain benefits (e.g., a code-review nudge that also slightly improves documentation) has a favorable ROI that single-domain evaluations would miss. |
| **Practical implementation** | The 2×2 institutional context framework is directly implementable as a decision tool: any A-Tech product team deploying a behavioral nudge can run the four diagnostic questions (incentives? social capital? competing interventions? attribution?) to predict whether cross-domain spillovers will arise in their user community. The four-mechanism taxonomy provides the theoretical scaffolding for explaining observed spillover patterns in product analytics. |
