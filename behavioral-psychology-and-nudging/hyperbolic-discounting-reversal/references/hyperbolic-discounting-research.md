# Hyperbolic Discounting: Foundational Research

## Core Definition

Hyperbolic discounting is a time-inconsistent model of delay discounting and one of the cornerstones of behavioral economics. It describes the tendency for people to prefer smaller, immediate rewards over larger, delayed rewards — and for this preference to reverse as time passes.

## Key Papers and Sources

### Ainslie (1975) — The Original Specious Reward
Ainslie, G. (1975). Specious reward: A behavioral theory of impulsiveness and impulse control. *Psychological Bulletin*, 82(4), 463–496.

- Introduced the hyperbolic discounting function as an alternative to exponential discounting.
- Showed that preference reversal over time is a fundamental feature of human choice, not an anomaly.

### Laibson (1997) — Golden Eggs and Hyperbolic Discounting
Laibson, D. (1997). Golden eggs and hyperbolic discounting. *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 112(2), 443–478.

- Applied hyperbolic discounting to savings behavior and consumption smoothing.
- Demonstrated that present bias leads to under-saving and over-consumption.
- Introduced the concept of "commitment devices" as a rational response to time-inconsistent preferences.

### Samuelson (1937) — Exponential Discounting Baseline
Samuelson, P. A. (1937). A note on measurement of utility. *Review of Economic Studies*, 4(2), 155–161.

- Established the standard exponential discounting model against which hyperbolic discounting is compared.
- Exponential discounting assumes time-consistent preferences; hyperbolic discounting shows they are not.

### Thaler & Shefrin (1981) — The Planner-Doer Model
Thaler, R. H., & Shefrin, H. M. (1981). An economic theory of self-control. *Journal of Political Economy*, 89(2), 392–406.

- Framed the self as containing a "planner" who values the future and a "doer" who values the present.
- Explains why hyperbolic discounters seek precommitment devices to bind the doer.

## Why It Matters for Marketing

| Phenomenon | Marketing Implication |
|---|---|
| Present bias | Monthly subscriptions feel smaller than lifetime purchases |
| Preference reversal | Buyers regret subscriptions over time; lifetime buyers rarely regret |
| Time inconsistency | Users who choose monthly today may prefer lifetime if asked in 6 months |
| Precommitment demand | Users *want* tools to help them make better long-term decisions |

## Key Insight for A-Tech

Developers are trained to optimize for efficiency but remain human in their temporal preferences. A-Tech's mission of ownership directly confronts hyperbolic discounting. The skill of reversal is not manipulation — it is choice architecture that aligns the user's present actions with their stated long-term values.
