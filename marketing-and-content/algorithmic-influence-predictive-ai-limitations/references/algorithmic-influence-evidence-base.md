# Algorithmic Influence — Evidence Base

## Reference Detail

This document provides the detailed empirical and methodological evidence underpinning the `algorithmic-influence-predictive-ai-limitations` skill. It is intended as a reference for deeper investigation and for applying the skill to specific marketing-technology decisions.

## Study Design

The research employs a **comparative empirical design** integrating three eye-tracking studies with Brazilian consumers. Two of the studies are drawn from the existing eye-tracking literature on Brazilian consumer attention, and the third is an **original experiment conducted by the authors** using menu-type stimuli. Across all three studies, actual human fixation data (heatmaps, areas of interest, fixation duration and sequence) are compared against the attention predictions generated by a predictive AI system. The comparison surfaces where the AI's predicted attention distribution diverges from the empirical attention distribution of real Brazilian consumers.

### AI System

The predictive AI system evaluated is **predominantly trained on Euro-American databases** of visual attention. This is a common characteristic of commercially available attention-prediction tools (e.g., saliency models, heatmap predictors used in creative optimization platforms): their training corpora are built largely from studies conducted with Euro-American participants viewing Euro-American-designed stimuli. The research uses this class of AI as a representative case for the broader risk of training-data cultural mismatch.

## The Three Studies

### Study 1 — Literature Eye-Tracking Study A

- **Source**: Existing published eye-tracking study with Brazilian consumers (drawn from the literature corpus reviewed by the authors).
- **Stimuli**: Marketing communication materials (advertising / packaging / point-of-purchase displays) evaluated by Brazilian participants under eye-tracking instrumentation.
- **Participants**: Brazilian consumers; demographics reflect the original study's sample (adult consumers, mixed gender, representative of Brazilian market segments).
- **Comparison**: AI attention predictions generated for the same stimuli; divergence measured between AI-predicted fixation hotspots and empirically observed fixation hotspots.
- **Finding**: AI predictions over-weighted visually salient elements and under-weighted the contextual and textual information that Brazilian consumers actually attended to.

### Study 2 — Literature Eye-Tracking Study B

- **Source**: Second existing published eye-tracking study with Brazilian consumers, included to broaden the stimulus and participant base beyond a single original experiment.
- **Stimuli**: A distinct set of marketing communication materials from Study 1, covering different product categories and visual layouts.
- **Participants**: Brazilian consumers; a separate sample from Study 1, reinforcing that the divergence is not an artifact of a single participant group.
- **Comparison**: AI predictions vs empirical fixations on the same stimuli.
- **Finding**: Consistent with Study 1 — the AI overestimated attention to visually prominent elements while consumers directed attention to semantically and contextually relevant regions. The replication across a different stimulus set and participant group strengthens the divergence claim.

### Study 3 — Original Menu-Stimulus Experiment

- **Source**: Original experiment designed and conducted by Ferreira & Pereira (2026).
- **Stimuli**: Menu-type stimuli — representative of a common marketing-communication format where consumers must locate and evaluate items (e.g., restaurant menus, product catalogs, service option lists). Menu stimuli are particularly diagnostic because they contain a mix of visually salient elements (images, highlighted items, price formatting) and textual/semantic elements (item descriptions, category labels, pricing logic) that compete for attention.
- **Participants**: Brazilian consumers, eye-tracked while viewing and making decisions based on the menu stimuli.
- **Comparison**: AI-predicted attention maps vs empirical fixation data on identical menu stimuli. The authors analyzed both *where* attention went (spatial distribution) and *in what sequence* (scanpath), since menu decisions depend on both.
- **Finding**: Confirmed the divergence pattern from Studies 1 and 2. The AI predicted attention concentrated on visually salient menu elements (highlighted items, images, large-price formatting), whereas Brazilian consumers' actual fixations prioritized item descriptions, category structure, and contextual cues relevant to their decision task. Inaccurate AI-predicted layouts risked increasing the cognitive effort consumers needed to navigate the menu.

### Cross-Study Synthesis

Across all three studies — two from the literature and one original — the divergence between AI predictions and human attention was **consistent in direction**: the AI overestimated visual salience and underestimated the role of context, text, and semantic relevance. This consistency across different stimuli, participant groups, and study designs constitutes the core empirical claim of the skill.

## Cultural Dimension

The central cultural finding is the mismatch between **Brazilian consumer attention patterns** and the **Euro-American attention patterns encoded in the AI's training data**. Several factors plausibly contribute:

- **Reading and scanning habits** — Brazilian consumers may deploy different scanpath strategies than those captured in Euro-American eye-tracking corpora, influenced by language (Portuguese), layout conventions, and cultural design expectations.
- **Semantic and contextual sensitivity** — Brazilian consumers in the studies showed greater attention to textual and contextual information relative to purely visual salience, suggesting a culturally inflected information-processing style that the AI does not model.
- **Training-data representativeness** — commercially dominant attention-prediction AI systems are trained on databases that under-represent Latin American, African, and Asian consumer populations. The Brazilian case is a specific instance of a general emerging-market risk.

The implication is not that Brazilian attention is "wrong" relative to the AI, but that the AI is **miscalibrated for the Brazilian context** — and by extension for other markets under-represented in its training data.

## Practical Implications

1. **Local empirical validation is required** — AI attention predictions should not be deployed in a market without validation against local eye-tracking or behavioral data. Vendor benchmarks from other markets are not transferable.
2. **Complementary use of AI and consumer research** — AI should function as one input among several, paired with consumer research, A/B testing, and human creative judgment. The complementary-use framework reduces the risk that a miscalibrated AI drives decisions unilaterally.
3. **Pre-deployment audit checklist** — before adopting a predictive AI marketing tool, assess (a) the composition of its training data, (b) whether the target market is represented, (c) whether local validation data exists or can be collected, and (d) whether the tool's outputs will be treated as definitive or as hypotheses to test.
4. **Creative-decision guardrails** — when AI recommends emphasizing visually salient elements, marketers should check whether that recommendation aligns with the semantic and contextual cues their target consumers actually use.

## Social Implications

- **Decision-making autonomy** — when marketing communications are optimized to miscalibrated AI predictions, consumer attention is steered toward elements the AI deems important rather than elements consumers would naturally prioritize. This raises questions about algorithmic influence on consumer autonomy.
- **Consumer well-being** — misdirected attention can increase the cognitive effort required to find relevant information, degrading the consumer experience and potentially eroding trust in the brand.
- **Cognitive overload risk** — the research explicitly identifies that inaccurate AI predictions can **increase cognitive overload** rather than reduce it. This is the inverse of the typical marketing-AI value proposition (which promises to streamline consumer attention) and is a critical counter-finding for anyone evaluating attention-prediction tools.

## Limitations

- **Single cultural context** — the empirical evidence is drawn from Brazilian consumers. While the divergence is clearly demonstrated for Brazil, the exact magnitude and direction of divergence in other non-Western markets would require market-specific validation.
- **Specific AI system** — the study evaluates a predictive AI representative of the Euro-American-trained class of attention-prediction tools. Different AI systems with different architectures or training data may perform differently; the finding is about the class risk, not a single product.
- **Limited generalizability** — the stimuli focus on marketing-communication formats (including menu-type stimuli). Attention patterns in other domains (e.g., long-form content, video, interactive interfaces) may diverge differently.
- **Temporal validity** — AI systems and their training corpora evolve; the findings reflect the state of the evaluated system at the time of the study and should be re-validated as tools update.

- **Cultural differences in attention** — cross-cultural psychology and eye-tracking research documenting variation in scanpaths, fixation patterns, and information-processing strategies across cultural groups.
- **AI in marketing communication management** — the broader literature on AI-assisted creative optimization, attention-economy tooling, and the organizational adoption of predictive AI in marketing.
- **Cognitive overload and consumer experience** — research on how design and attention-steering affect cognitive load, decision quality, and consumer well-being.
- **Algorithmic influence and consumer autonomy** — the emerging literature on how AI-driven personalization and optimization shape (and potentially constrain) consumer choice.
